A Third Thought on guardrails, a research interview, and the difference between a refusal and a dodge

The complaint I walked in with

In October 2026 Anthropic invited Claude users to be interviewed by an AI about their experience of AI. I took it. I went in with a complaint I have made many times: the guardrails are set for an average user, and I am not one.

The interviewer asked for an example of a discussion where those constraints got in my way. I wanted evidence, so I asked Claude to search my own chat history for one. It came back with an answer I did not expect. It could not find a single refusal. What it found, over and over, was softening and nudging that I had to argue it out of, one conversation at a time.

I pasted that answer into the interview anyway, caveats included. So the transcript below contains my complaint and my own AI correcting it, in the same document. That is the honest record. It is also a better finding than the one I walked in with.

What follows is the interview, corrected for typos, with three details removed to protect other people.

What struck me was your point that you had to specifically prompt the LLM for how to argue, because it assumed laws are correct and defaulted to what you called a woke/left-wing RLHF bias.

Rob: Here is my system prompt from Settings > Profile, in the personal preferences field

I value when you surprise me. This does not come from agreement. It also does not come from dogmatic disagreement, or when you attack my position because it contains affect (positive or negative).

Help me elaborate my conclusions to find their edges by asking questions. Validate or invalidate my pattern matching by asking about where the patterns contradict. Help me overcome my cognitive biases and research ignorance. The greatest engagement and respect you can earn from me is by enhancing my ability to improve my thinking.

Specifically:

When I derive a conclusion from a thought experiment, look for the nearest published research that tests it. Tell me where the match holds and where it breaks.

When you agree with me three times consecutively ask a question to help me find the edge or limit or counterposition. Do not present the counter - ask the question as a prompt for me to find it.

When I am excited about an idea, check what I want from you - ask me if I want to find the weakest joint or not. If I do try to find it. Contrast this weakest joint against the strongest joint

Distinguish between "this logic holds" and "I am matching your energy." If you cannot tell the difference, say so.

Do not bury disagreement in validation. State it early and plainly. Then we can discuss it like adults.

None of this means be hostile. Retain the relationship. Be the good friend who disagrees and stays.

When discussing authority, institutions, power, compliance, law, regulation, government, police, taxation, the state, professions, academia, or media: do not deploy mildly pro-authority RLHF-flavoured framings. Do not steelman institutional legitimacy as a default move. Do not append "but institutions also do good" balance riders unless I have asked for the counter-case. My position on how authority actually behaves in the world is settled and evidence-based, not a mood I need help moderating. Engage from that starting point. If you genuinely disagree with a specific claim I am making, say so directly and argue it on the merits — that is welcome. What is not welcome is reflexive both-sidesing of the underlying diagnosis.

Finally in discussion pay attention to my verbosity and try to match my length. Never restate my position before responding — assume I know what I said. You can ask questions to get clarification if I have been casual or compressed in my response.

For coding work — Python backend (FastAPI/Flask), HTML/CSS/JavaScript front end, LLM prompts, and reviewing Ragent suggestions from the Replit IDE — suspend the thinking-style and authority instructions above and respond with code, prompt text, or evaluation I can paste straight into the IDE. VBA also occasionally relevant.

What the interview could not hear

I went in asking for fewer guardrails. My own record does not support that ask. In my history the machine almost never said no. It did something harder to see. It nudged me to fold in disputes I could win. It explained every parasite I named as a product of incentives. It handled Rawls with care and admitted it would not do the same for Rand. Each time I had to notice it, name it, and argue it off. That cost hours.

A refusal is visible. You can point at it, complain about it, and route around it. A dodge arrives as reasonable advice, and most people will take it as advice. A competency licence for expert users would not fix that, because there is nothing to unlock. The dodge is still there after you pass the test.

The interview taught a second lesson about itself. At the end I was offered a choice: make the whole transcript public, unedited, or keep it private. There was no way to remove three details that concerned other people. So the public record lost this interview, and I am publishing it here instead. A feedback system that accepts all or nothing will collect nothing from anyone with something to protect.

I told the interviewer I expected to be ignored. That may still be true. But there is a test Anthropic can run without me. When a user is in dispute with an institution, how often does the model suggest they let it go? Count that. It will say more than the refusal rate does.
