A Capital Idea, Just in Time

At 2am on a Sunday I watched a YouTube explainer on David Harvey's Seventeen Contradictions and the End of Capitalism. It did not explain two of them well. I think Harvey is a Fuckwit, but I wanted to be able to argue his case anyway. One of the most important steerings in Paragentism is the counterfactual. I can think Harvey is a Fuckwit, but I am one too if I don't steelman his case so well that his supporters would agree I fully get where he is coming from.

Two days later I had stopped steelmanning him. Harvey had become something more useful: a muse. Reading him made me ask a question I had been avoiding for years.

Why, after forty years of trying, am I still a worker?

The line that matters

Marxists split the world into labour and capital. Labour trades time for money. Capital invests money to make money. Harvey thinks the split is unfair: workers are paid less than the value they create, and capitalists keep the rest.

I don't buy the theory underneath that. The labour theory of value, the idea that value comes from the work put in, was set aside by mainstream economics in the 1870s. A mud pie that took hours to make is still worthless. But the split itself is real. It just isn't really about class. It is about time.

A worker's earnings are capped by the hours in their day. A capitalist's are not. Capital earns while its owner sleeps, multiplies with every worker it employs, and compounds when the surplus is reinvested. Successful capital pulls away from successful labour, not because capitalists work harder, but because their earnings are not tied to their hours. Thomas Piketty compressed it to r > g: once wealth is large enough, its returns outrun anything work can earn.

That is the line that matters. Not owner versus employee. Earning with your time versus earning without it.

USP prices everything

How is anything valued in a market? I use USP. Not unique selling proposition, but utility, scarcity and perception. A thing is worth what it does, how hard it is to get, and how valuable people believe it is.

USP prices labour too. Capital buys workers like any other input, and most workers score badly on all three. Their utility is replaceable. Low-wage labour is not globally scarce. And capital perceives workers as expensive and unreliable. So capital employs when it must, pays the minimum it can, and automates whenever it can. Forcing wages up by decree makes that perception worse and speeds up the automation.

USP prices jobs as well. Workers perceive secure jobs as scarcer and more valuable than they are, so they cling to the one they have. Mobility and reinvention are concentrated in a minority. Most people would rather keep a job than retrain for a better one.

And USP explains what shits me about modern business. Instead of raising utility, by making things better or cheaper, or reducing scarcity, too many firms exaggerate utility, manufacture scarcity and manipulate perception. I wrote about one small case in Sharp Practice. It is extraction, not creation.

Writing this, I realised that idealistically avoiding extraction has made me a poor marketer. The P in USP is a skill set: effective promotion. How do you reach enough customers, get them to believe in your offer enough to buy it, and still leave a net margin after cost of goods sold that more than covers your capital investment over time? I am still learning the answer, despite winning the medal for best student in my MBA. I'm pretty sure the marketing I was taught was bound up in academic Fuckwittery that missed some necessary practicalities. Or maybe it was just incomplete. Or maybe I plain missed the key bits. My MBA defined marketing as "the system of activities that get the right product at the right time, price and place to the right customers." My corporate reality at the time was closer to "marketing is how firms trick their customers into making poor purchase decisions in the firm's favour."

Where Harvey is right

Harvey's anger is real, and some of it is earned. Capital that failed in 2008 kept earning after the bailouts. Credit warps what people can buy and why they buy it. Marketing sells workers things they don't need at prices they can't afford, while their wages are held down. That makes it harder for workers to save the surplus they would need to become capitalists. Harvey says the system is stacked against workers accumulating capital and for capital holders accumulating more. On that, I agree with him. And as a worker, I have been a victim of all of it over my working life.

Where he goes wrong is the remedy. Harvey and the Left want more control: higher wages by decree, more transfers, a bigger welfare state, more government. Control needs a bigger state, and the state is the ultimate extractor. It takes by fiat, not by exchange, whatever it can take without revolt, and it takes far more from labour than from capital. More state to fix extraction is more Fuckwittery to fix Fuckwittery. I have also been taxed by the state far more than I think is reasonable. I recognise the irony: whining about the tax rake is the same move as Harveyites whining about low wages.

Harvey also teaches workers that their fate is set by the system. Locus-of-control research finds that people who believe outcomes are in their own hands search harder for work, earn more and recover better. A theory that tells workers they are trapped helps keep them there. Something in me is programmed not to submit to that learned helplessness, and staying angry about it is part of how. I think workers largely have themselves to blame for not becoming capitalists and getting ahead.

Was I a worker all along?

Here is the uncomfortable part. Run my own life through this and I come out a worker who has failed in forty years to get ahead, despite my concerted best efforts.

I have had eleven careers: scientist, salesperson, manager, entrepreneur, lecturer, CX consultant, management consultant, director, AI programmer, sales manager, performance manager. I have started more than seventy ventures. Exactly one ever earned without my time, and I was forced out of it. Every other venture turned my hours into income. That is a worker's model with an owner's title.

My accumulated financial capital is two properties. The equity is locked. I can release it only by selling, which is slow and expensive, or by borrowing against it, which adds debt.

My human capital is enormous. My network is not. Run USP over myself: utility high, scarcity high, perception low. Perceived value travels through networks. Mark Granovetter found most opportunities arrive through weak ties, not close friends. An innovator without a network is a good product nobody has heard of.

So the honest answer to the question Harvey made me ask: I have been a high-paid worker, with low income security, and a chronic failure as a capitalist. I traded time for money very well. I never built enough that earns without me.

Diagnosing my anger at not getting ahead, I had completely missed USP and earning independent of time as critical considerations. My ventures were aimed at economic independence. Their business models were fundamentally flawed for getting there, and I didn't have the financial capital, networks or marketing skills to pull them off. Maybe I also wasn't lucky enough to be in the right place at the right time with the right opportunity to overcome those gaps.

But I have grit, intelligence, creativity and experience, and more financial capital, a better network, greater skills and a better idea of what to invest in than at any time in my life. I can resent the system for not propelling me to success already, or I can pick myself up, stop whining and get on with it. Meanwhile, a bit more about where that system misses the mark for most of us. It's not about the controls.

The agentic answer: innovation

Innovation creates new work in domains where automation doesn't yet exist. Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo call this the reinstatement effect. David Autor and colleagues found that about 60% of US jobs in 2018 were in job titles that didn't exist in 1940. In new work, workers are useful, scarce and worth paying for. Their USP rises without anyone setting a wage by decree.

The same innovation devalues sunk investment. Capital likes innovations it owns and hates innovations others own, because they strand what it has already built. Kenneth Arrow showed in 1962 that incumbents have less incentive to innovate for exactly this reason. A fast-innovating economy erodes the head start accumulated capital holds over newcomers and workers alike.

And as the frontier widens, it opens niches where people with skills and relationships can create surplus and become capitalists themselves.

Innovation doesn't need anyone to stop being self-interested. People are self-interested to the point of greed, and everyone wants something for nothing. No system Harvey designs will change that. Innovation harnesses it instead of fighting it.

It asks something of everyone. The state has to get out of the way, with lower taxes and regulation that loosens for individuals and tightens with scale. Capital has to accept that its sunk investments will be devalued. Workers have to become mobile and invest in their own skills.

Innovation shrinks what winning capitalists enjoy by revaluing their accumulated assets. It disadvantages them against capitalists with no sunk costs, and it makes workers more valuable, at least for a while. It also makes extraction harder for the state, for a time, because innovations often sit outside its grasp until it changes the laws, and that takes time.

What I am doing about it

Surplus has to be reinvested in something that earns without my ongoing time. That is the whole game.

So I am building a software business designed from day one to run without me, sold through partners who bring the network I lack. Property can come later, once the house debt is gone and this year's tax is saved.

I may fail again. Most new ventures do. But I have finally named the line I need to cross. It is not employee to owner. It is time to no time.

Harvey did not give me an answer. He gave me the question. Thanks, Harvey, you Fuckwit.
