Four Weeks’ Notice

Tonight my Didi driver told me something worth more than the fare.

He started driving part-time as a student. Then he landed a manager’s job and kept driving on the side for a bit of extra money. Eventually he decided the job had to go. It wasn’t his boss. The job had simply grown too big and too hard, and it stopped being worth the effort and the discomfort. He’d planned to line up a new one before leaving, but it didn’t come through fast enough. Most people in that spot have two bad options: stay and stew, or walk out and burn the bridge.

He did neither. He gave four weeks’ notice, finished properly, and went back to driving the next day.

That’s what agency looks like in practice. It isn’t a grand gesture. It’s a fallback he set up quietly and kept running when nothing seemed to be paying for it. The account was live, the car was ready, and the habit was already there. An option you only open on the day you need it isn’t much of an option.

Now the onus is on his old employer. If they want the next manager to stay, they have to make the job worth staying in: better pay, a better-shaped role, or less grind. Nobody negotiated that. His leaving sent the message.

I’m a pretty hard-core capitalist, and this is the mechanism I want for balancing the trade between labour and capital. Albert Hirschman said you have two responses to a bad deal: exit or voice. Unions are voice. They work by lobbying for those who stay, and the research shows they reduce quitting. My driver had no union. He had something a union can’t give: the ability to leave on his own terms. Every worker should have that, and the leverage that comes with it. When people can move, employers have to compete to keep them.

Compare the floor most people actually have: the dole. You queue for it, you prove you’re needy enough to get it, and you keep proving it. It pays you not to work, then makes you show you’re trying to find work. It shrinks you while it holds you up, and I’d guess it does your self-esteem no favours either.

The Didi floor works the other way. There are no shifts, no minimum hours and no required location. Every fare is a real person who actually wants to get somewhere, so the income is earned rather than handed over. He decides how much he earns by choosing how, when and where he logs on. He comes out better, Didi gets a driver, I get a ride home, and the taxpayer pays nothing.

Nobody designed this as welfare policy. It came out of a market where someone had something to sell and someone else wanted to buy it. We tried to build the same thing through government and got Work for the Dole: compulsory, attached to a benefit, and shown to keep people on payments longer. It isn’t even real work. It’s closer to the community service handed out to offenders as punishment.

Is it guaranteed? No. But the risk runs the other way from what we’re told. Didi only profits when drivers keep turning up, so it has to stay worth their while. Centrelink needs no such reason. It routinely cuts off payments for a missed appointment or a late job-search form, and the person it cuts off has nothing else to fall back on.

The third thought: there’s plenty of demand beyond driving, and the trades are short of hands. Picture a platform for trades assistants: a builder posts the hours, and you take as many as you want today. Then make TAFE trade training free for the trades we’re actually short of. That means electricians, not a Cert IV in modern dance. Count the logged site hours toward the qualification. No apprentice wages that keep you on the breadline for three years, and no subsidies for employers to rely on. Every assistant gets a real road to a trade, travelled at their own pace.

That’s the difference between help that gets you back on your feet and help that keeps you on your back: acute, not chronic. A trade is a one-off investment that pays the person for the rest of their working life. The dole is a payment that never ends and builds nothing.

Will the people who run the current system build this? I doubt it. They’re the ones it would make unnecessary. But the question isn’t whether a better floor is possible. One just drove me home. The question is why we keep funding the worse one.
