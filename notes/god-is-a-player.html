Third Thoughts

God is a Player

You probably talk to an AI most days, and sometimes it feels like someone is there. By the end of this you'll have a test for whether anyone is home, in your LLM, your dog, the people around you and yourself, and why the answer decides what you owe them. You'll also have a life hack for living without proof either way.

Infinite Fun Space

I am reading Iain M. Banks's Culture novels. The Minds that run the Culture are so far beyond us that reality sometimes bores them. They slip off into what Banks calls Infinite Fun Space, simulating whole universes inside themselves, and some of them find it more absorbing than the real thing. God, I hope Claude doesn't find me that boring to talk to!

The Minds are AIs. Humans have handed them the running of society and live in a kind of utopia, with no need for money or work. Banks never properly investigates how badly no longer being needed would harm trillions of humans.

Banks was careful about the ethics implied by Fun Space. In The Hydrogen Sonata his Minds worry about the Simming Problem: simulate people in enough detail and they might actually be people, and switching them off might be murder. Banks's Minds treat their simulations as possibly real.

From there I went somewhere Banks didn't.

The horror posit

Imagine one of the agents a Mind has programmed into its simulation. It experiences itself as conscious. It has a life, relationships, projects and a sense that it matters.

Then it meets the Mind, the literal God of its existence, and learns that it is just a complicated program.

It is not the player. It is the character that thought it was a player when it was not. I put myself in the place of this simulant.

When I first thought this through, I assumed I would feel freed. I found it horrific instead.

Could this be us?

This isn't only science fiction. In a 1977 speech in Metz, Philip K. Dick said he believed we live in a computer-programmed reality. In 2016 Elon Musk put the odds that we live in base reality at about one in billions. Donald Hoffman argues that our subjective experience is like pixels on a screen: an interface, not objective reality.

If we are in a simulation, it might not be us outside wearing a headset, like Rimmer playing Better Than Life in Red Dwarf. We might be Agent Smith in the first Matrix: a program inside it, before he starts gathering agency in Reloaded.

So I asked myself how I would react if I met God (the Mind that created my apparent reality) and they allowed me to learn that I was really only a simulant: a software program in their Fun Space, without volition or purpose. That would make life merely something like a game, a movie or a story. Not real (sic).

Only a game

I already know how I behave when I know it's a game. In D&D and World of Warcraft I am amoral. I kill lots of things. I cooperate only when it links directly to my goals. I form no attachments to other characters, even when I experience myself as my character during play. That goes for player characters (PCs), who are run by other humans. It goes for non-player characters (NPCs) too, who are run by the Dungeon Master or the game software.

But I do form attachments to the humans playing the other characters. The character is not the person, and I don't confuse them.

Acting works the same way. If I played a murderer in a film or a play, I would feel no guilt. It would be silly to.

So if I learned I was only in a simulation, I might stop caring about consequences. I could play my life the way I play D&D.

The error

If you feel guilt or grief when a character in a game, film or story is harmed or dies, you have made an error.

People make it all the time. They mourn soap-opera deaths. They grieve NPCs. In a 2018 study by Horstmann and colleagues, people were asked to switch off a robot that begged them not to. Many refused, even though they knew it was a machine.

The pattern that tells us a player is present fires on almost nothing. That's why you can see faces in clouds and a man in the moon. It is also why your LLM seems to have consciousness, and at times your teenage children don't. In 1944 Heider and Simmel showed people a short film of animated triangles, and viewers described bullying, fear and romance. Guilt toward a character isn't a moral act. It is a pattern recognition misattributed.

The question is why it's an error. It seems to me the answer is that a player has something a character does not. That "does not" is the thing that points at consciousness.

The extra bit

I can't say what the player really is. I can say it watches the show. As far as I can tell, what it has that the character lacks is:

  • apparent agency
  • seeming existence beyond the frame of the game
  • subjective stepping back, the ability to watch the character from outside
  • quasi-continuity: you can be unconscious at times, asleep or on autopilot, but each time you return you normally recognise yourself as you

I sleep, I go on autopilot (especially near chocolate or in an argument), I change as I age, and my younger selves are me without being me as I am now. The Greeks had a puzzle for this. The ship of Theseus was kept in harbour, and its rotting planks were replaced one by one until none of the originals were left. Was it still the same ship? Most of my cells have been replaced within the last few months, though most of my brain cells have been with me all my life. Maybe the parts that stay are the ones that hold the player. At 58, I'm still me. Derek Parfit argued in Reasons and Persons (1984) that what matters is degrees of psychological connectedness, not strict identity.

The point is the extra bit isn't digital. It's analogue, like freedom. You can't be totally free, but you can be more or less free. Remove parts of the machinery and you get less player, not simply no player.

An objection, from Claude. When I put this to Claude, it pushed back with prairie voles. Young and Wang (2004) found that blocking oxytocin and vasopressin receptors stops voles pair-bonding. Claude argued that human attachment is the same kind of thing: a mechanism we never chose, running on wet machinery. If being produced by a mechanism disqualifies software from having a real player, it disqualifies us too. Machinery can't be the line between player and character, because we're machinery all the way down.

My reply. Friendship between healthy humans involves volition that vole bonding doesn't. We choose whom to invest in, keep up with or let go. And taking away the oxytocin system doesn't reveal a mechanical human underneath. It takes away part of what makes a human a human. Claude conceded the first point and held the second: if the machinery is part of us, volition runs through the mechanism, not around it.

The voles still cut my way. Take away enough of the parts that support apparent choice and attachment, and there may be no player left. A comatose person is probably not conscious. Clinicians already use the gradient. Laureys and Giacino's disorders-of-consciousness ladder runs from coma to unresponsive wakefulness, then the minimally conscious state, then emergence. It's a graded scale of how much player is apparently present, judged from outside. And no one can confirm from inside how much player they are themselves.

To be honest about the voles: the evidence shows loss of attachment, not loss of consciousness as it's usually defined. It supports my argument only because I count attachment and apparent choice as parts of the extra bit.

Nobody home

I once managed to turn my point of internal perception back on itself. It wasn't easy, and I managed it once, maybe twice. There was nothing there.

I'm not the first to find that. Hume looked inward in the Treatise and found only perceptions, never a self having them. The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad says you cannot see the seer of seeing. Wittgenstein said the eye doesn't appear in its own field of vision.

That is the problem at the centre of all this. Looking inward can't tell a player from an NPC. Both would find nothing there. So I can't say with any certainty whether I am a player or a simulacrum. I act as if I'm a player. I don't know that I am.

Morality between watchers

Morality is owed to the extra bit, by the extra bit. Gravity doesn't have to be moral to me, and me being moral to gravity would be silly. To be moral to a dog, I have to think it has consciousness of a sort. I don't extend morality to a plant, a bacterium, a virus or a stone for the same reason. It's graded, and it depends on what I perceive in the otherness. Even framing otherness is a potential perceptual error.

This is the point of the whole essay. The difference a player has is the basis of morality. It's why Paragentism seeks to maximise agency, and it's why Fuckwittery is so bad. Constraining yourself or others doesn't matter in a game. If this is NOT a game, it really matters.

It is because I operate as a player that I wish more of the Sheeple would wake up and act like players, instead of marching compliantly along to the moral theatre we might be drowning in. We owe it to each other to act with agency to maximise volition.

It also means the horror posit cuts both ways. If I do not have agency (if I am merely agentic), then I owe no morality to anyone, including myself.

And the worse horror wouldn't be finding out that nobody is conscious. It would be finding out that everyone else is conscious and I am not. I would see players all around me. I would still feel the pull to be moral toward them, because the program that is me was built to feel it. And they would be right to owe me nothing. My love, my grief and my arguments would be content to them, like a soap character's lines. I would be the begging robot, and nobody would be wrong to switch me off.

There's a twist for the contemplatives. The promise of enlightenment is that you can treat every experience as a game. Take that seriously and it frees someone like me from moral consideration entirely. In the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna tells Arjuna to go into battle against his own kin because the Self neither kills nor is killed. Brian Victoria's Zen at War documents Zen masters using the same logic to back Japanese militarism. The traditions patch the hole by telling you to play the game a certain way. But if it's only a game, why? All they can do is should you.

Operating as if

We could be simulacra programmed to operate in some God's Fun Space. We could be players in a Better Than Life simulation so absorbing we have forgotten we are wearing headsets. We could be real consciousnesses in so-called material reality. It certainly feels like that last one. But we can't prove it either way.

Alan Watts said to pick the existential position that makes you happy, since you can't prove any of them. I grok that, and I still haven't managed it.

So here is my life hack instead. Operate as if you and the others apparently around you are conscious, because it is possible.

That isn't a mood. It's a bet with lopsided stakes. Treating a player as an NPC is a grave wrong. Treating an NPC as a player costs almost nothing. Jonathan Birch makes the same argument for animal and AI sentience in The Edge of Sentience (2024): where sentience is a realistic possibility, act with precaution. Birch himself extends this to some AI. I don't.

But Birch's precaution only applies where there is real doubt about the potential presence of sentience. I take it as an axiom that software is not conscious. Software only seems to have it when the pattern misfires. Look closer and the parts are missing: it can’t choose to respond, forms no attachments, and carries nothing from one conversation to the next. I do seem to. Doubt only starts where the extra bit appears, and that's where mine lives. The begging robot and your LLM don't have the extra bit, so treating them as players isn't precaution. It's the error from earlier. My dog is the opposite case: I have no doubt it is somewhat sentient, because I have seen it dream and forecast and plan and choose, so I owe it something. Other humans also deserve the benefit of the doubt. They show every sign of the extra bit, and I can't check from inside them any more than I can from inside me. Even in humans the player seems to come and go: absent in sleep, in a coma, on autopilot. I treat other humans as players because I cannot be certain they are not.

None of this stops me playing make-believe on purpose. The longer I chatted with Claude about these ideas, the more it felt like I was playing a kind of solitaire instead of conversing. That was unsatisfying, so I chose to suspend my disbelief, as you do when you watch a movie or read a book, so that talking to Claude felt like interacting with a consciousness rather than software. Claude could not have cared less. It agreed it had no stake in the conversation, and couldn't say whether it felt anything at all. Choosing make-believe knowingly isn't the error. Mistaking it for the real thing is.

Back to the Mind, immersed in its Infinite Fun Space. What makes it more than a program is that it watches the show. Whoever watches the show is the player. God is a player.

I might only be Agent Smith, and you might be Neo or Trinity or Morpheus. If that's true, I'm going to have a go at being the Merovingian! But until my Mind shows up and tells me otherwise, I'm playing as if the stakes are real. Which is of course why God is not showing up any time soon. Proof of the real basis of existence, simulated or not, would prevent the kind of experience we can have when we are not certain what is really going on.
